1

On real digits, max-softmax is much sharper than soft-WTA. At 500k, all three mnist runs have nearly the same pre-inhibition response: median peak 0.67, about 28 effective neurons. After inhibition the median effective count (sum/max) is 18.3 for soft-WTA, 6.3 for β = 10 and 2.6 for β = 20. The peak is kept in every case. A real digit's runner-ups trail the leader by 0.1–0.3, so exp(−β·gap) suppresses them strongly.

2

Max-softmax raises weak responses, as the identity predicts. The peak response to shuffled digits is low (median 0.075) with small gaps. At β = 10 the inhibitor raises 98% of the neurons, and the effective count goes up from 30 to 38 instead of down. At β = 20 it raises 20%, for an effective count of 29. Uniform noise (peak 0.21) is not raised; its gaps are large enough. So a flat, weak response stays below its own peak but is made more uniform. Whether "true to inputs that have no strong response" should mean that, or "left as it was", is a design decision. The gap scales with the activity level, so β must be chosen relative to the levels the group actually produces. The 1–5% neuron noise sets a gap floor below which β only amplifies noise.

3

Training with a different inhibitor barely changes what the group learns. Pre-inhibition trajectories of the three runs agree to about 0.01 in peak and mean. The drop in the shuffled-digit response happens between 300k and 400k in all of them. Winner-by-digit NMI is 0.050–0.053 and all 50 neurons win in every run. The reason is the winner rule (hd_ngm2_neuron_group.cpp, step 3). It picks the first neuron, by index, whose output reaches a threshold drawn from U(0.8·max, max). Both inhibitors keep the max and are monotone, so they only change how many neurons sit in that top 20% band: 2–4 for soft-WTA, 1 for max-softmax. Replaying the draw on the recorded responses, the strongest neuron wins with probability 0.64–0.73 (soft-WTA), 0.87–0.91 (β 10) and 0.94–0.96 (β 20). These configs have a single group, so nothing downstream reads the inhibited output. Testing local inhibition's effect on learning needs a multi-group config.

4

The winner rule has an index bias. Because the draw takes the first neuron in the band, low-index neurons win more often whenever several are in it. At 20k the mean winner index under soft-WTA is 8.6, against 17.5–20 with max-softmax, where the band usually holds only the leader. The bias is independent of the inhibitor and is worth fixing on its own, e.g. by choosing uniformly among the neurons in the band.

5

On chairs3d no inhibitor can act, because the group saturates before inhibition. The images are mostly white background: input mean 0.95, 94% of pixels above 0.5. The dendrite response is roughly the matched fraction of input mass, so every neuron matches. At 500k, 86% of the group outputs for real chairs are clamped at exactly 1.0, the lowest is 0.97, and the effective count is 50 for every run. Ties have gap 0, so max-softmax leaves them untouched at any β; a ratio-based kind fails the same way. The saturation is visible from the first checkpoints (peak 0.90 at 500 steps, 1.00 by 100k). Replaying the winner draw, neuron 0 wins 98–99.6% of real chairs under all three inhibitors. The winner weight is σ(1 − out), and out ≈ 1 here; I did not measure how much learning that leaves. In the checkpoints neuron 3, not neuron 0, has the most-changed permanences, which suggests its lead was set before saturation. Only uniform noise (peak 0.60) leaves gaps, and there max-softmax works as on mnist: effective count 48 → 41 (β 10) and 35 (β 20). The fix for chairs3d belongs upstream, in the input encoding or the dendrite response (e.g. inverting the background or normalising by expected mass), not in local inhibition.
