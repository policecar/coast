Dendrite Noise Gate

An ngm2 dendrite decides from its input statistics whether the input is noise. It uses the normalized Shannon entropy (NSE) of the input and the input's mean activity, in two places:

gate(nse, avg) = 1 − σ((nse − 0.8) / 0.2, w) · (1 − σ(avg, {0.5, 0.5})) response get_response() w = {0.25, 0.5} nse, avg = minimum over the dendrite's input channels learning adapt_synapses() w = {0.5, 0.5} per input channel

The response gate scales every segment's activity; the learning gate scales every synapse's permanence update. This page measures what the gate does on the recorded probe inputs of six datasets, how NSE behaves as a measure, and what changes when a centre-surround retina sits in front of the dendrite.

What the measurements show

1

On raw pixels the gate cannot see structure. Shuffling an image's pixels keeps its value distribution, so NSE, mean activity and the gate are the same for real and shuffled digits (gate separation −0.04 to +0.05 on mnist, emnist and kmnist). The trained groups still separate the two perfectly (+0.99 to +1.00). That comes from the learned connectivity and the synapse penalty, not from the gate.

2

What the gate does on raw pixels is flag dense, flat input. Uniform noise gets a response gate of 0.61 against 0.89–0.96 for digits. Removing the gate lowers real-vs-noise separation from +0.92 to +0.69 on kmnist and from +1.00 to +0.98 on mnist (estimated). It misfires elsewhere: real cifar10 images get a lower gate (0.50) than noise (0.61), density-matched dsprites noise passes (0.95), and for bright inputs the 1 − σ(avg) factor switches it off entirely (chairs3d: 0.999).

3

NSE is not a sparsity scale. With the datasets' 0–5% input noise it is not monotonic: at 4096 inputs a pattern with 1% of the entries active scores 0.90, 5–10% active scores 0.82, and 50% active scores 0.93. A thin edge looks as noisy as half the image switched on. The window also moves with input size: without noise, NSE 0.9 means 51% active at 784 inputs but 39% at 12288, and with noise the curves for 784 and 12288 inputs lie up to 0.07 apart. Of the alternatives only mean/max rises monotonically. Hoyer sparsity also dips, at 2–3% active, but depends far less on input size (at most 0.02 apart).

4

Behind a retina, the same gate detects structure. Centre-surround filtering is spatial, so shuffled pixels turn into dense, high-contrast ON and OFF maps. Judged jointly, as get_response does by taking the minimum over channels, the gate separates real inputs from shuffled pixels and from uniform noise at +1.00 on mnist, kmnist, dsprites and chairs3d (k = 0.8), and at +0.85 / +0.91 on cifar10, which no measure separated on raw pixels. The window is still placed for raw digits: real cifar10 images are gated to 0.33, and dsprites at k = 1 to 0.47.

5

Judged per channel, the weak polarity is rated as noise. The learning gate judges each input channel on its own, by design: one input may carry a signal worth learning while another carries only noise. At k = 0.8 the mnist OFF channel learns at 0.015 of the rate of its ON channel (0.99); on chairs3d it is ON that learns at 0.006 against OFF at 0.88. A real digit's OFF channel is a faint halo on the noise floor, and NSE rates it noisier than the OFF response to uniform noise (separation −1.00). No other per-channel measure in the table rates it correctly against uniform noise either. This is the late "shadow" in training. Whether the halo should count as signal is a question of the retina's gain more than of the gate.

6

The two gates differ in steepness, not position. Both centre at NSE 0.9. Going from 10% to 90% of its transition, the response gate spans NSE 0.84–0.96, the learning gate 0.87–0.93.

7

No first-order statistic helps on raw pixels; after the retina several work. Judged jointly, NSE and Hoyer separate almost equally well; Hoyer needs two sums instead of a logarithm per entry and depends less on input size. Subtracting the noise floor before NSE fixes the weak channel against shuffled input but not against uniform noise, and it collapses to 0 once more than half the entries are active.

Options for the dendrite

  1. Keep per-channel learning, give the weak polarity more signal. Learning is judged per channel on purpose. If the faint halo should be learned, raise it above the noise floor at the retina (gain, or lower noise) instead of changing the gate. The two windows could still share one steepness.
  2. Put the retina in front of every dataset. The gate then measures spatial contrast instead of value spread, and it separates structure from noise on every dataset tested. The 1 − σ(avg) term was written for raw intensities and needs a second look.
  3. Replace NSE by Hoyer sparsity. Same separation behind the retina, cheaper (two sums instead of a logarithm per entry) and nearly independent of input size. It also dips for very sparse inputs, and the window has to be placed again.
  4. Drop the gate on raw inputs. Learned connectivity already rejects shuffled input; the gate's remaining contribution on raw pixels is a modest push against dense, flat noise.

How the numbers were made

  • Probe inputs and responses: ngm2 groups trained 500k steps on each dataset with the shipped configs; probes are frozen steps with a pass-through inhibitor, 2000 inputs per condition. "Shuffled" permutes each image's pixels; "uniform noise" is U(0, 1) per pixel (dsprites: density-matched binary noise).
  • Gates computed with float32 mirrors of sigmoid and normalized_shannon_entropy. The "without gate" peaks divide the recorded output, minus the mean neuron noise 0.03, by the response gate; this ignores clamping, so chairs3d (outputs clamped at 1) is not meaningful there.
  • Retina: a Python mirror of retina::contrast_filter_t, checked against the C++ filter on 100 mnist and 100 chairs3d inputs at k = 0, 0.7 and 1 (largest difference 7·10⁻⁷).
  • Separation is 2·AUC − 1 over all pairs. Retina sections use 500 inputs per condition.
